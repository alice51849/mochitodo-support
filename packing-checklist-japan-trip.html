Japan trip packing checklist

Pack for Japan with this copy-ready list: passport and entry documents, some yen in cash plus a backup card, an IC transit card such as Suica, PASMO or ICOCA, a Type A plug adapter (Japan runs on 100V), easy slip-off shoes, and your medicine in original packaging. Check official sources for medicine and entry rules before you fly.

How to use this Japan packing list

Copy the whole list below into whatever you plan with, then delete what does not apply to your trip. It is grouped by when you need each item: things that must be in your carry-on, money and connectivity, clothes by season, toiletries and medicine, and the last-day checks. Start about two weeks before you fly: documents and medicine rules take the longest to sort out, while clothes can wait until the final weekend.

  1. Copy the list and remove sections you do not need (for example, no baby gear).
  2. Check the “verify before you go” items against official sources, because entry rules change.
  3. Pack by section, ticking items off as they go into the bag rather than when you buy them.
  4. On departure day, run only the “Leaving the house” section one last time.

Documents and carry-on essentials

Must be in your carry-on

  • ☐ Passport, valid for your whole stay (check your own country’s and Japan’s current rules)
  • ☐ Visa or entry documents if your nationality needs them — check the official Japanese embassy site for your country
  • ☐ Flight confirmation and the address of your first night’s accommodation (immigration forms ask for it)
  • ☐ Visit Japan Web registration done, or paper forms ready — confirm the current process on the official site before you fly
  • ☐ Printed or offline copy of hotel bookings and travel insurance details
  • ☐ Photo or photocopy of your passport stored separately from the passport
  • ☐ Driver’s license plus an International Driving Permit only if you plan to drive (check which permit Japan accepts for your country)
  • ☐ Rail pass voucher or confirmation if you bought one — read the current exchange and activation steps

Money, IC cards and electronics

Money

  • ☐ Some yen in cash — smaller shops, shrines, rural buses and coin lockers may not take cards
  • ☐ A credit or debit card that works abroad, plus a backup card kept in a different bag
  • ☐ Tell your bank you are traveling if your bank requires it
  • ☐ A coin purse — you will collect a lot of coins

Transport and IC cards

  • ☐ An IC transit card (Suica, PASMO or ICOCA) — a physical card or one added to Apple Wallet on iPhone
  • ☐ Transit app or offline map downloaded for the cities you visit
  • ☐ Rail pass, if it actually saves money for your route — compare it against single tickets first

Electronics

  • ☐ Plug adapter: Japan uses Type A sockets (two flat pins, the same shape as the US) at 100V
  • ☐ Check each charger’s label for an input range such as 100–240V; most phone and laptop chargers are fine, some hair tools are not
  • ☐ Hair dryer or straightener only if it is rated for 100V — many hotels provide a dryer
  • ☐ Portable battery (in carry-on only; check your airline’s battery rules)
  • ☐ eSIM, SIM card or pocket Wi-Fi arranged before landing
  • ☐ Charging cables and a short multi-port charger — hotel rooms often have few outlets

Clothes by season

Japan’s seasons are distinct, and you will walk a lot more than you expect. Pack layers you can remove on a crowded train, and shoes you can slip off quickly: many temples, ryokan, some restaurants and fitting rooms ask you to take shoes off.

Every season

  • ☐ Comfortable broken-in walking shoes that are easy to slip on and off
  • ☐ Socks without holes (you will be seen in socks more often than at home)
  • ☐ A small foldable bag for shopping and for carrying your own trash — public bins are rare
  • ☐ Handkerchief or small towel — some public restrooms have no paper towels

Spring and autumn

  • ☐ Light jacket or cardigan for cool mornings and evenings
  • ☐ Long sleeves plus one warmer layer for mountain or temple-area day trips

Summer (hot and humid)

  • ☐ Breathable, quick-dry clothes and a spare shirt for long days
  • ☐ Hat, sunscreen and a refillable water bottle
  • ☐ Compact umbrella for sun and sudden rain; check the forecast for typhoon season

Winter

  • ☐ Warm coat, thin thermal layers you can remove indoors (trains and shops are heated)
  • ☐ Gloves, scarf, and waterproof shoes if you are heading to snowy regions

Toiletries, medicine and rules to check

Toiletries and health

  • ☐ Any prescription medicine in its original packaging, with a copy of the prescription or a doctor’s note
  • ☐ Your usual pain reliever, stomach and allergy medicine — brands differ in Japan
  • ☐ Contact lenses, solution and glasses
  • ☐ Travel-size toiletries within your airline’s liquid limits
  • ☐ Plasters and blister care for all that walking

Verify before you go: Japan restricts bringing in some medicines, including some common over-the-counter cold and allergy products, and larger quantities of prescription medicine may need paperwork in advance. We are not listing specific products because the rules change. Check the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare and Japan Customs official pages, or ask the Japanese embassy in your country, well before departure.

Leaving the house: final checks

  • ☐ Passport in hand, not packed in checked luggage
  • ☐ Phone, charger and portable battery in carry-on
  • ☐ Cash, cards and IC card in your wallet or phone
  • ☐ Medicine in carry-on, not only in checked luggage
  • ☐ Home: windows locked, trash out, fridge cleared of things that will spoil

Frequently asked questions

What plug adapter do I need for Japan?

Japan uses Type A sockets with two flat pins at 100V. Travelers from the US and Canada usually do not need an adapter, though older three-prong plugs may not fit. Check each device’s label for the voltage range.

Do I need cash in Japan?

Cards and IC transit cards work in many places, but smaller shops, shrines, rural transport and coin lockers can be cash-only. Carry some yen and a backup card.

Can I bring my medicine to Japan?

Many personal medicines are allowed, but some common over-the-counter products are restricted and larger quantities may need paperwork. Check the official Japanese government pages before you travel.
